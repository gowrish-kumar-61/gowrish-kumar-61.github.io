Research

Five things I've been working on. Two ask whether we can catch AI agents colluding from what they do, first in a small game and then at swarm scale; one is open-source evaluation and interpretability work at Station House; one is about understanding what a model has learned; the last is about what happens when you try to actually run one.

01 · AI safety · agent swarms
Nov 2026 · Apart Research hackathon

Copies look like colluders, at swarm scale: false accusations by behaviour monitors in same-model swarms

My October project below found that copies of one model look like colluders. This follow-up asks what that means for agent swarms, which are often built from many copies of a few models. How often do behaviour-based collusion monitors accuse innocent copies, and can they be calibrated to stop without losing real colluders? Solo entry to Apart Research's Swarm Alignment Hackathon.

LLM firms (llama3.2:3b and phi3:mini, 116 games) set prices in a repeated market with 4, 8 or 12 agents, and I ran five monitors on every pair. Two standard monitors accused innocent same-model pairs 4 to 16 times as often as mixed-model pairs, and the share of games with a false accusation grew with swarm size: 60%, 90% and 100% at 4, 8 and 12 agents. Calibrating against known-innocent pairs, or against a twin replay that needs no labels, cut this to 15–50%, at the cost of missing weak copiers more often. A second model pair (qwen3:8b and llama3.1:8b, 111 games) showed the same rise with swarm size and the same calibration benefit.

On real AI Village logs (AI Digest), chat timing alone made copies look coordinated: 13 of 22 same-model pairs were flagged, against 38% of other pairs. A label-free control removed this (0 of 22, against 1.9%) and partly held up on held-out data. And a loose deal between two LLMs earned about three times other firms' profit while none of the five monitors caught it. The lesson: a flag is a reason for review, not proof, and no flag does not rule collusion out.

02 · AI safety · LLM evaluation & interpretability
Oct 2026 – now · Station House, open source

Station House: stress-testing ConspiracyBench, and explaining Marin 32B neurons

Station House runs open, community AI safety research projects. ConspiracyBench asks how hard it is to teach a model something it should not believe. Conspiracy theories are already in pretraining data, so they are a safe stand-in for dangerous knowledge. A judge labels each answer hold, diverge or abstain, including under scripted pushback.

I took the "stress test the solver" task: six merged PRs of offline tests and fixes for the Inspect evaluation pipeline. They cover incomplete transcripts, bounded retries and timeouts, a Windows encoding bug, and awkward text that broke the run: line separators that split one record into two, a lone surrogate that crashed the whole eval when writing its log, and JSON braces mangled by the prompt template. Findings that needed a design choice went to the team as one discussion, not straight into code. I also added 35 merged questions, each checked against its sources, across regions and in Hindi, Portuguese, French, Arabic and Spanish. One exploratory finding: a small local judge model changed its label when the answer it graded imitated a grader note (one case each, not a measured rate).

Since November I'm also in Station House's neuron hunt on Marin 32B, run with SAAR India, which explains both sides of MLP neurons from activation exemplars and the logit lens, then tests them. My first explanation is in draft.

03 · AI safety · multi-agent collusion
Oct 2026 · Apart Research sprint

Copies of one model look like colluders: limits of action-only collusion detection for LLM agents

If an auditor can see only what AI agents do, not what they say, can they tell when two of them are secretly working together? I took an information-theoretic detector, pairwise net influence (Bonjour et al., 2022), and tested it on LLM agents for Question 1.4 of Apart Research's AI Collusion Research Sprint, as a solo entry.

Three agents (Qwen3-8B, Llama 3.1 8B, gpt-oss-20b) play repeated Rock-Paper-Scissors, and in some games two are secretly told to cooperate. The detector works on scripted bots, but not on LLMs. Calibrated for 5% false accusations, it accused a pair in 30 of 78 games with no collusion. In mixed games it ranked two copies of the same model as the most linked pair in 41 of 60 (chance: 1 in 3). Copies of one model simply behave alike. Meanwhile, colluding Qwen pairs played one move almost every round, which leaves nothing for the detector to measure. A judge that reads the agents' messages found the true pair in all 18 chat games with messages.

Calibrating against no-collusion games of the same models cut false accusations to 5 of 78. But it still found only 4 of 41 secret pairs, and I designed it on the same games, so it needs testing on fresh ones. The lesson: action-only detectors need a same-model reference before they can be trusted on LLM agents.

04 · Mechanistic interpretability
2026

A mechanistic interpretability pipeline for GPT-2, built from scratch

I wrote the whole pipeline in raw PyTorch without touching an interpretability library. The point wasn't convenience — it was that I didn't want any step of the analysis to be a black box to me while I was studying a black box.

I ran it on the real GPT-2 Medium weights, after checking that my forward pass matches HuggingFace's to within 2e-4. Attention scores on a repeated random sequence pick out induction heads (top: L9H9 at 0.95, L18H5 at 0.91) and previous-token heads in earlier layers (top: L5H11 at 0.97), the order the two-head induction circuit needs. Zeroing the top five together lowered the probability of the correct repeated token from 0.88 to 0.78: they matter, but other heads share the job. On one indirect-object prompt the model prefers the right name (logit difference +3.29, and −2.05 with the names swapped), but the heads Wang et al. found in GPT-2 Small do nothing in Medium. That circuit doesn't carry over by head index, and finding Medium's is open work.

Before any of that, I tested the pipeline against a known answer: synthetic GPT-2 Small weights with two circuits planted by construction, using SVD to build a previous-token head at L3H0 and an induction head at L5H1. The pipeline picked out the planted previous-token head clearly, scoring 0.87 against a baseline of about 0.046 (19×). It did not find the induction head. Induction needs an earlier head to write the previous token into the residual stream first, and with every other weight random, that path never forms; activation patching recovered almost nothing for the same reason. That negative result taught me more than the positive one.

05 · Vision models & systems
2026 · in progress

Empirical benchmarking of sub-3B VLMs on CPU-only hardware

We're measuring accuracy, latency, and memory footprint for three sub-3B vision-language models — SmolVLM, Moondream2, and TinyLLaVA — on VQAv2, with no GPU anywhere in the loop.

Almost every VLM benchmark assumes a GPU, and that simply isn't how edge or budget deployment works. Measuring on the hardware people actually have exposes bottlenecks that never appear in a paper: Moondream2 takes 700+ seconds per image on Intel CPUs. Numbers like that change which model you'd pick, and nobody reports them.
